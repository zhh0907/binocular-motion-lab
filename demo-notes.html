Pronounced movement, fixed parameters

This page documents an engineering demonstration. The cases are movement patterns, not confirmed lesions, and have no preset NIHSS scores. The demo does not read or modify the main simulator’s patient library.

Why the impairment stays consistent

u = r + 2(r - q)
C = 45c = 5.4°
If d·u > 0: u_out = d·C·tanh(d·u/C)
Otherwise: u_out = u
τ·dq/dt = u_out - q

r is the normal model’s reference eye position, q is the current patient eye position, and d is the fixed impaired direction. Capacity and direction stay constant; there is no per-frame random noise. The drive function and its first derivative are continuous at zero. For fixed r, the right-hand side decreases with q, giving a stable equilibrium. Drive saturates smoothly in the impaired direction rather than clipping eye position at a particular angle.

These checks establish internal directional consistency, continuity and numerical stability, not human physiological validity. The affected axis also passes through the response dynamics in the opposite direction, potentially adding delay. Vertical and other unaffected channels preserve the normal reference exactly.

A shared observation task

The automatic target stays at a depth of 0.70 m and moves slowly to ±0.36 m horizontally, approximately ±27.2°, holding each side for 1.5 seconds. Motion segments use the quintic curve 10a³−15a⁴+6a⁵, with zero velocity and acceleration at hold boundaries. This is a slow following demonstration, with no additional saccade examination. Eye position in the impaired direction approaches 5.4°, making the difference from the normal reference visible.

Live camera mode uses the same model and accepts arbitrary valid 3D targets. RGB depth is assumed, and the camera is not clinically calibrated. The eye views face the observer. The unmirrored camera preview aligns the finger, orange target marker and eye movements on screen. Tracking loss freezes eye position and internal state. Changing cases, restarting or switching input modes resets state; identical inputs and initial state reproduce identical results.

What the medical evidence supports

Bronstein and colleagues’ clinical, oculographic and MRI study of horizontal gaze palsy and internuclear ophthalmoplegia supports distinguishing conjugate gaze impairment from adduction deficits. An MRI case report of an abducens nucleus lesion documents horizontal conjugate gaze palsy. These studies support directional patterns, not the demo’s c, τ, k or tanh parameterization.

The adduction template does not model the abducting nystagmus or separate convergence pathways that may be relevant to INO. The abduction template does not fully represent resting eye alignment and other signs of abducens nerve palsy. These patterns therefore cannot be treated as validated patients with specific neural lesions.

The official NIHSS instructions base scoring on examination findings. Pronounced limitation does not automatically mean a score of 2. This demo provides no reflex examination evidence and assigns no score. Clinicians must assess plausibility, evidence sufficiency and any eventual score.
